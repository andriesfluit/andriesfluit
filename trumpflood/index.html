Methodology

How the zone is decided, where the data comes from, and what to be skeptical about.

Daily collection

Three times a day — 08:00, 14:00 and 20:00 Belgian local time — a script fetches headlines from 31 Belgian RSS feeds: Google News BE (Dutch & French general), 8 Google News BE topic feeds (politics, world, business, tech, sport in NL; politics, world, business in FR), and 21 direct outlet feeds spanning VRT NWS, HLN, De Standaard, De Morgen, Het Nieuwsblad, GVA, HBVL, Knack, Sporza, Bruzz on the Dutch side, plus RTBF, La Libre, L'Echo, DH, 7sur7, BX1 directly, and De Tijd, Le Soir, Sudinfo, L'Avenir and RTL through Google News' site: filter (their direct feeds sit behind Cloudflare). De Standaard's direct feed is reached through the cloudscraper library to handle the basic Cloudflare JS challenge. Articles whose pubDate is today (Belgian local date) are kept; duplicates across feeds are removed by URL.

Core vs. wide tier

Not every feed is equal. Brussels-only outlets (BX1, Bruzz), sport-only outlets (Sporza), and the Google News aggregator feeds (which repackage content we already pull directly) structurally dilute the denominator with stories that are local, single-subject, or duplicated. To avoid that skew, the headline number on this page uses a core tier: national and regional-generalist outlets only — VRT, RTBF, De Standaard, De Morgen, HLN, Het Nieuwsblad, GVA, HBVL, Knack, La Libre, L'Echo, DHnet, 7sur7, plus De Tijd, Le Soir, Sudinfo, L'Avenir and RTL (reached via Google News' site: filter because their direct RSS is Cloudflare-gated). The full wide corpus is still computed and stored in data/log.json as a cross-check. Today’s core corpus: 759 headlines, 12 Trump matches = 1.6%.

Including every feed we fetch — Brussels-local (BX1, Bruzz), sport-only (Sporza), and the Google News aggregators that repackage outlets we already pull directly — the full wide corpus is 1002 headlines with 22 Trump matches = 2.2% (+0.6pt vs. core). The cross-check exists so we can spot a day where the two tiers diverge sharply, which usually signals an aggregator artefact rather than a real shift. The 7-day rolling average of the core share is 2.13%.

Trump match

Each headline title is scanned for Trump references in three languages (Dutch, French, English), case-insensitive, whole words only. That means both the literal name and the common indirect references Belgian outlets use to refer to the current US administration:

  • Direct name — trump (matches “Donald Trump”, “Trump Jr.”, etc.).
  • The White House — white house, witte huis, maison blanche / maison-blanche; plus oval office and bureau ovale.
  • The US / American president — US president, American president, Amerikaans(e) president, president van de VS, président(e) américain(e), président(e) des États-Unis (accents optional).

A headline like “Het Witte Huis waarschuwt Europa” counts even though the word “Trump” does not appear in it. This closes the largest honest gap in the old literal-name-only detector. The comparator counts used for dominance and rank are a separate mechanism and still match people by name only — expanding Trump’s pattern while the other nine figures stay name-only would bias the rank. No fuzzy matching, no body text (titles only).

Comparators & themes

The same headlines are scanned for nine other people (Putin, Macron, De Wever, Bouchez, Orbán, Meloni, Netanyahu, Zelensky, Musk — with Trump as the tenth reference entry) and for fourteen broad subject categories (war, crime, EU politics, Belgian government, etc.) using multi-language regex (NL + FR + EN keywords) so “klimaat” and “climat” both count as Climate.

Zone assessment

No single number can honestly say “Trump is flooding Belgian news today”. The zone combines four signals, each answering a different question. Every zone above Dry requires several of them to clear a floor simultaneously — a single-signal spike is never enough.

1. Share — “How much of the news is about Trump?”

The percentage of today’s Belgian core-tier headlines that reference Trump. Computed as Trump headlines ÷ total headlines × 100.

Today: 12/759 = 1.6%. A low number means Trump simply is not in the news much, regardless of what other signals say.

2. Dominance — “Is he THE figure of the day?”

Trump’s name-only mentions divided by the sum of mentions of the nine other named figures we track (Putin, Macron, De Wever, Bouchez, Orbán, Meloni, Netanyahu, Zelensky, Musk). A value above 1.0× means Trump alone out-mentions the other nine combined.

Today: 0.46×. This catches the “slow domestic day” case where Trump scoops up all named-figure attention even when coverage volume is modest.

3. Breadth — “Is it everywhere, or one paper?”

The fraction of core outlets that published at least one Trump headline today. Only outlets with ≥ 5 headlines count toward the denominator so a near-empty feed doesn’t skew the ratio.

Today: 47% of core outlets ran a Trump story. A high dominance combined with low breadth means one outlet is obsessing; a high breadth means Belgian newsrooms collectively decided Trump deserves a spot today.

4. Rank — “Is he on top at all?”

Trump’s position among the ten named figures, by mention count. Acts as a veto: higher zones require Trump to be #1 or #2. If he’s #5 on a day everyone is talking about Macron, no zone upgrade follows.

Today: rank #1 of 10. For context, Trump would rank #10 of 15 against broad subject themes (war, crime, EU politics, ...) — but themes bundle dozens of stories so a single person rarely beats them. Theme rank is context, never a zone driver.

Deviation (context, not a hard gate)

Today’s core share divided by the 14-day median. Requires at least 7 prior live-day records, which we only build up gradually. Until then this signal is treated as “pass”.

Today: — (not enough live history yet). The 7-day rolling average of the core share is 2.13%.

The thresholds are calibrated against our core-tier RSS corpus, which is narrower than GDELT’s full Belgian feed. In practice that means 2.5% of our core headlines being about Trump is a meaningful signal of dominance; the same percentage in GDELT’s corpus would be routine. Once we have 30+ days of live history, these absolute thresholds can be replaced by percentiles of Trump’s own history (“Flooding = top-10% day ever recorded”), which is the only truly self-calibrated version.

Caveats & limits

  • Title-only matching. Titles are scanned, not article bodies. Indirect references that the expanded detector does not cover (“the administration”, “Washington”, “POTUS”) are still missed. Smaller undercount than before the NL / FR / EN expansion, but non-zero.
  • Trump-the-family. The regex also matches “Trump Jr.”, “Eric Trump”, “Trump Tower”. Usually ≤2% noise but it’s there.
  • Time-of-day sampling & peak-of-day rule. Fetches run three times a day (08:00 / 14:00 / 20:00 local). RSS feeds only expose the latest N items, so an afternoon fetch may show fewer Trump headlines than the morning one because earlier pieces have scrolled off. To avoid understating a Trump-heavy day, we keep the peak observation for each day: if a later run sees a lower share than the one already stored, we keep the earlier record and only update the “last checked” timestamp. Later runs still replace the record when they see a higher share, so a late-breaking Trump surge is captured.
  • Dedup policy. URL duplicates (same article reached via two feeds, e.g. Google News republishing an HLN link) collapse to one globally. Title duplicates only collapse within the same outlet — a normalised title (lower-case, accent-stripped, editorial prefixes like “VIDEO.” removed, first 80 chars compared) that repeats inside one publisher’s feed becomes one. But if HLN, Nieuwsblad and GVA all run the same Reuters headline, those are three editorial decisions and all three count. That is what “breadth” measures: publishing choices, not unique authored stories.
  • Aggregator overlap. The Google News feeds (only used in the wide tier, not core) republish outlets we already pull directly; URLs differ from the direct ones so some duplication leaks in on the wide number.
  • Belgian press covers world. The denominator is heavy with international news that Belgian outlets choose to publish. This measures “share of the news Belgians read” — not “what Belgian society is discussing internally”.
  • Stale comparator list. The ten-person comparator set is fixed. If a new Belgian political figure surges in coverage, Trump’s rank against this list will look artificially high. Periodic review needed.
  • Arbitrary absolute thresholds. The 5.0% / 3.5% / 2.0% / 0.8% cutoffs plus the 2.0× / 1.2× dominance and 60% / 45% / 30% breadth floors are chosen rather than calibrated against historical distributions. As the archive of live days grows, these should be re-expressed as percentiles of Trump’s own history.
  • Theme overlap. A headline can match multiple themes (a Gaza article counts for War and for EU politics if Macron is quoted). Themes are not mutually exclusive.
